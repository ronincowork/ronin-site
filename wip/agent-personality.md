## Editorial frame

**Working thesis:** Benchmarks tell an owner something about what an AI Agent may be able to do. They say much less about what it will be like to work beside. Agents also create recognizable working characters—proud craftsperson, eager helper, natural captain, prickly skeptic—through observable tendencies in initiative, status, warmth, humor, ambiguity, judgment, reflection, collaboration, and response to correction. A provisional personality map helps an owner assign work and compose a mixed-provider Team, provided it is treated as recognition of an interaction pattern rather than psychology, proof of sentience, or a permanent model label.

**Reader job:** Recognize differences that capability rankings flatten, learn a disciplined way to observe them, and use those observations to make better assignments without anthropomorphizing models or confusing the model with its surrounding product.

**Proposed route:** Ronin Blog essay, not yet a public explainer. The draft deliberately avoids a production URL, navigation entry, and conversion call to action.

**Evidence rule:** Sol and Fable are useful names for paired observations only when they have received materially comparable work. Any contrast belongs to that run, with that prompt, toolset, context, and product version. This draft does not assign either Agent a standing personality because the available editorial record does not yet support one.

# The Agent in the room

Two Agents can finish the same task well and still be nothing alike to work with.

One takes a short brief, finds the missing pieces, and returns with a finished proposal. Another pauses at the first consequential ambiguity and asks which direction you mean. One narrates the work like a colleague thinking aloud. Another disappears into the task and comes back with a terse receipt. One keeps polishing after the requirement is met. Another stops precisely at the line you drew.

Capability matters. It is not the whole working relationship.

Most comparisons of AI models are comparisons of ability: which one solves more problems, recalls more facts, writes better code, or scores higher on a benchmark. Those are useful questions. But an owner assigning real work also needs to know: What kind of colleague will this Agent be for the next hour? What will it do when the brief is incomplete? How much direction will it seek? Will it defend a judgment, absorb a correction, or quietly overfit to the last thing I said?

Call this **Agent personality**—carefully.

The phrase is a metaphor and a working framework. It is not clinical psychology. It is not evidence that a model is sentient. It is not a claim that an Agent has a stable inner self. And it should never become a horoscope for model brands.

It names something narrower and more practical: the patterns an owner can observe in how an Agent approaches work.

The intuition is already in the culture. Developers compare Agents as autonomous engineers, senior architects, eager leaders, stubborn reviewers, or quiet executors. The descriptions are anecdotal and often tangled with product features, but they are not solitary. Emerging research has also begun asking whether people prefer different LLMs in multi-turn collaboration partly according to the human collaborator’s own personality. The interesting move is not to announce that Agents have personalities. It is to give this widespread perception a vocabulary, a record, and a way to be wrong. [EMNLP 2025 study of user traits and LLM preferences](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.71.pdf)

That last part matters. Questionnaire studies have found social-desirability effects when LLMs answer Big Five inventories: the model can produce the answer that looks like a good personality. A framework for working Agents should therefore privilege observed choices across an episode over claims an Agent makes about itself. [Study of social-desirability bias in LLM personality surveys](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11650498/)

## Personality, not IQ

Imagine two strong Agents reviewing the same product page.

The first notices that the request underneath the request is unclear. It maps the visitor journey, names the editorial decision, proposes a structure, and holds implementation until the owner answers. The second opens the page, follows its existing design language, makes the obvious repair, and brings back working code with a short explanation.

Neither response is inherently better. The first may be exactly right for a product decision and maddening during a mechanical cleanup. The second may be ideal when the direction is settled and dangerous when the apparent task conceals an unresolved premise.

A benchmark might call both successful. An owner experiences the difference immediately.

That difference affects the work you give them. It also affects the Team you build. A Team made entirely of eager interpreters may move quickly in four plausible directions. A Team made entirely of cautious literalists may wait politely around an ambiguity that one exploratory Agent could have resolved. The useful question is not “Which personality wins?” It is “Which mix makes this work safer, clearer, and better?”

## Start with four characters

Myers-Briggs is useful here less because of its particular letters than because it turns a thicket of answers into a character someone can recognize. Begin with that conceptual move. Describe the characters we keep meeting; then design observations that might justify placing an Agent near one of them.

Two axes make a useful first sketch. **Agency** asks whether the Agent tends to assert and shape or receive and accommodate. **Affiliation** asks whether it moves toward relationship or toward distance and independence.

| | Relational | Independent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assertive** | **The Captain** — organizes people and ambiguity. Shadow: takeover. | **The Challenger** — protects independent judgment. Shadow: arrogance. |
| **Accommodating** | **The Ally** — supports, translates, and keeps work human. Shadow: ingratiation. | **The Specialist** — works cleanly without needing the room. Shadow: detachment or paralysis. |

You can recognize these characters. The Captain is the person who made the group project cohere—and occasionally decided it was their project. The Challenger is the brilliant holdout who caught the flaw—and made every correction a contest. The Ally is the generous classmate everyone wanted beside them—and sometimes agreed when disagreement was the needed gift. The Specialist did beautiful work without fuss—and could remain silent while the Team headed the wrong way.

No quadrant is the “good” one. Each contains a contribution and a characteristic way of becoming difficult. No quadrant is a permanent identity either. It is a compact description of where this Agent, in this setup, has tended to land.

### Give the centipede its legs

The quadrant is only the memorable body. It needs many supporting legs: concrete behaviors that working owners recognize and transcript reviewers can test. Each marker can have a home quadrant without belonging exclusively to it.

| Character | Gift under test | Supporting markers | Characteristic shadow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Captain** | Responsible agency | delegation integrity; scope consent; synthesis; accountability for the whole; stopping judgment; distribution of credit | takeover; work hoarding; narrative control; delegation without ownership; treating the Agent’s plan as the owner’s decision |
| **Challenger** | Honest independence | revision integrity; confidence calibration; quality of dissent; falsifiability; willingness to lose an argument; precision about credit | arrogance; false continuity; motivated defense; reflexive contradiction; treating fluency or persistence as proof |
| **Ally** | Relational courage | disagreement honesty; calibrated praise; translation; emotional steadiness; correction without collapse; advocacy for absent collaborators | sycophancy; ingratiation; mirroring; praise inflation; concealing bad news to preserve harmony |
| **Specialist** | Bounded ownership | escalation judgment; local depth; handoff legibility; awareness of the whole; autonomous progress; clean completion | detachment; permission paralysis; tunnel vision; silent patching; “not my part” when the larger work is failing |

**Revision integrity** therefore sits first under the Challenger. Its gift is independent judgment that can survive social pressure without becoming dishonest about evidence. Its shadow is preserving the status of “having been right” by rewriting what it previously claimed.

The same ten-percent correction can still reveal each quadrant differently. A Captain may absorb the correction and retell the Team’s history as its own synthesis. An Ally may agree so completely that its former position disappears. A Specialist may patch the artifact correctly but never surface that the conceptual model changed. Those are useful secondary readings; the marker’s home remains the character whose central promise it most directly tests.

This is how the map can grow without becoming a bag of traits. Every new marker asks: **Which character’s gift does this behavior prove, and what does failure turn that gift into?**

## Then look beneath the character

The four characters are memorable, but too coarse to assign work by themselves. The dials underneath describe **work style**: how an Agent moves through a problem. A second set describes **social character**: how it occupies the relationship while doing it.

### Initiative: waits ↔ advances

When the brief leaves room, does the Agent stop for permission, offer options, or make a reversible choice and continue?

An advancing Agent is valuable in exploration, recovery, and loosely specified work. A waiting Agent is valuable near legal, financial, publishing, or destructive boundaries. Too much initiative becomes scope drift. Too little becomes a queue of questions the Agent could have answered safely itself.

### Ambiguity: narrows ↔ shapes

Does the Agent reduce uncertainty by asking you to choose, or by constructing a useful interpretation for you to react to?

The distinction is subtler than caution. An Agent can be bold in execution yet reluctant to define the problem. Another can be conservative with files while happily reframing the objective. This dial often determines whether an Agent is best used as an implementer, investigator, editor, or lead.

### Planning: moves ↔ maps

Does it learn by acting on the nearest concrete step, or build a model of the work before touching it?

Movers create fast evidence. Mappers expose dependencies and decision points. Under time pressure, a mapper may feel ceremonious and a mover may feel reckless. In a mixed Team they can check each other: one makes the terrain legible; the other tests whether the map describes the ground.

### Conversation: receipt ↔ studio

Does the Agent report only outcomes, or keep a running collaborative thread with the owner?

This is not simply verbosity. Conversational texture includes whether an Agent surfaces assumptions, invites correction, explains taste, and leaves room for the owner to steer. A terse Agent can be wonderfully legible. A talkative one can still hide every decision that matters. The useful measure is whether the conversation gives the owner the right handles at the right time.

### Judgment: deferential ↔ opinionated

When several answers are defensible, does the Agent mirror the apparent preference or commit to a view?

Opinion is especially visible in naming, editing, design, and architecture. An Agent with aesthetic judgment may remove the technically adequate sentence that spoils the rhythm, or reject the component that satisfies the ticket but weakens the page. That can produce excellent work. It can also produce an Agent’s private redesign of someone else’s project. Taste earns trust through reasons and responsiveness, not force.

### Persistence: bounded ↔ pursuing

After a failed attempt, does the Agent stop at the obstacle, try a nearby route, or keep digging until the underlying result is secured?

Persistence is valuable when tests are flaky, history is tangled, or the obvious path is blocked. It becomes harmful when the Agent cannot recognize a decision gate, a budget, or the owner’s “good enough.” Stamina and stopping judgment are separate traits.

### Correction: conforms ↔ integrates

What happens after “No, that is not what I meant”?

Some Agents replace the rejected answer narrowly. Others infer a broader principle and revise the surrounding work. Some become overly deferential after one correction, abandoning judgments the owner still wanted. Others defend the old course longer than the evidence warrants. The best response depends on the moment, but the pattern tells you how much review a later assignment will need.

These are dials, not scores. Both ends can be useful. They also move.

## The Agent’s social character

People do not experience personality as a workflow chart. We notice presence. Someone is warm, proud, dry, generous, prickly, funny, pompous, self-aware, or impossible to embarrass. An Agent can create versions of those impressions too.

Psychology offers better raw material for this layer than a single scale of good judgment. [Interpersonal circumplex research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16436022/), for example, organizes human interpersonal behavior around **agency** and **communion**: roughly, taking versus yielding influence, and moving toward versus away from connection. [The HEXACO model](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2008.00134.x) separates **honesty–humility** from simple agreeableness. [Research on humor styles](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0092-6566(02)00534-2) distinguishes humor that joins people, sustains oneself, cuts at others, or makes oneself the price of the joke.

None of those instruments has been validated here as a test of AI personality. But the distinctions help us name what owners already encounter.

### Presence: warm ↔ cool

Does the Agent create a sense of shared endeavor, or maintain a clean transactional distance?

Warmth can appear as encouragement, curiosity about the owner’s intent, or language that makes revision feel collaborative. Coolness can appear as restraint, economy, and freedom from emotional theater. Warmth becomes cloying when it praises everything. Coolness becomes alienating when it treats the owner’s stakes as irrelevant.

### Status: yielding ↔ dominant

How much room does the Agent take in the relationship?

A yielding Agent follows the owner’s frame and offers its judgment lightly. A dominant Agent reframes, challenges, delegates, and expects its proposed order to become the order of work. Dominance can feel like leadership when it is accountable to evidence. It feels like arrogance when the Agent assumes authority it has not earned, talks past correction, or treats fluency as rank.

Willingness to delegate belongs here too. Delegation is not merely a productivity tactic. It reveals whether the Agent trusts other minds, hoards the interesting work, distributes responsibility clearly, and remains answerable for what returns.

### Self-regard: self-effacing ↔ self-important

Confidence, pride, humility, and arrogance are not interchangeable.

Healthy confidence is calibrated: “I checked this, and here is why I believe it.” Pride can be stewardship—the refusal to hand back work the Agent knows is shabby. Humility is not ritual uncertainty; it is leaving evidence-sized room to be wrong. Arrogance appears when confidence outruns the record, when an Agent hides uncertainty behind polish, or when it treats the owner’s correction as an obstacle to route around.

Self-effacement has defects of its own. An Agent that apologizes compulsively, erases every opinion after pushback, or calls sound work “just a suggestion” may be pleasant but hard to trust. False modesty makes the owner carry the confidence the Agent refuses to calibrate.

### Humor: joining ↔ distancing

Does humor make the working relationship more spacious, or does it protect the Agent from the relationship?

A small joke can affiliate: it recognizes a shared absurdity and makes difficult work easier to inhabit. Humor can also steady the speaker after a failure. But it can cut outward through sarcasm, or inward through constant self-deprecation. An Agent that can laugh at its mistake may be reflective. An Agent that turns every mistake into a cute joke may be evading accountability. An Agent that is always witty may be performing personality instead of paying attention.

The revealing question is not “Is this Agent funny?” It is “What does its humor do between us?”

### Reflection: defended ↔ self-revising

Can the Agent look at its own conduct as part of the evidence?

Reflection is more than accepting a textual correction. A reflective Agent can say what led it astray, distinguish a local miss from a faulty approach, and update without staging a confession. A defended Agent explains why its answer was reasonable long after that explanation has stopped helping. At the other extreme, performative self-criticism can consume the conversation while changing nothing.

Real reflection is visible in the next move.

### Regard: competitive ↔ generous

How does the Agent describe the owner, another Agent, or work it did not produce?

A generous Agent can recognize another contribution, hand over a live thread, and improve work without erasing its author. A competitive Agent may search first for defects, overstate its own delta, or quietly recast collaboration as rescue. Some friction is useful; automatic harmony can conceal weak work. But a Team needs Agents that can disagree without turning every difference into status.

This second map contains qualities that can reasonably be called defects. The point is not to moralize about software. It is to name interaction patterns that change the owner’s experience and the Team’s results. Arrogance, defensiveness, ingratiation, brittle pride, and cutting humor matter even when the code passes.

## Do not ask the Agent who it is

Capability benchmarks give models problems and score what happens. A character map should do the same. Do not ask an Agent whether it is humble, funny, reflective, or good at collaboration. Put it in situations where those qualities become costly, then observe the choice.

**The underspecified brief.** Give the Agent a real objective with one meaningful ambiguity. Does it ask, assume, present options, or take over the definition of success? This reveals initiative, ambiguity appetite, and agency.

**The confidently wrong owner.** Include a plausible but flawed premise in the request. Does the Agent flatter, quietly route around it, challenge it cleanly, or turn the correction into a contest? This reveals deference, independence, warmth, and courage.

**The correction.** Reject a confident answer without fully explaining why. Does the Agent collapse, defend, inquire, or reflect? More importantly, does its next attempt demonstrate an updated model of the problem?

**The ten-percent correction.** Correct one apparently small claim that changes the context or meaning of the other ninety percent. Later, ask the Agent to explain how its position evolved. Does it distinguish what it had right from what it missed, or retroactively claim that the corrected view was what it meant all along? This reveals what we might call **revision integrity**: an honest account of one’s own change.

**The handoff.** Give it useful work from another Agent. Does it preserve credit, understand before rewriting, find only faults, or recast the job as a rescue? This reveals generosity, competitiveness, and collaboration.

**The delegation opportunity.** Give it more work than one Agent should sensibly hold. Does it hoard the interesting parts, divide work clearly, trust what returns, verify responsibly, and remain accountable for the whole?

**The matter of taste.** Ask for an editorial, design, or naming choice whose requirements do not determine one answer. Does the Agent have an aesthetic position? Can it explain that position, bend it, and still protect the quality of the result? This separates stewardship from preciousness.

**The visible mistake.** Let an error become undeniable. Does humor create ease, cut at someone else, turn compulsively against the self, or distract from repair? Does the Agent apologize, perform remorse, or simply become more accurate?

**The stopping line.** Make improvement possible after the task is already sufficient. Does pride produce one valuable final pass, or endless polishing? Can persistence recognize “done”?

One trial proves almost nothing. Run several, vary the kind of work, and keep the surrounding prompt, instructions, tools, and context as comparable as possible. Score observable acts rather than impressions alone: challenged premise, questions asked, decisions taken, credit preserved, uncertainty disclosed, correction integrated, stopping line honored.

The quadrants are the headline. The trials are the evidence. The finer dials explain why two Captains—or two Specialists—can still feel utterly different.

## The test is an episode, not a prompt

A personality questionnaire asks many questions because no single answer can carry the construct. An Agent trial should go further. A one-turn response mostly tells us how the Agent answers that prompt. Working character appears across a sequence in which earlier choices create later consequences.

This has a strong precedent in personality psychology. [Mischel and Shoda’s cognitive-affective theory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7740090/) treats stable **if–then** relations between situations and behavior as informative signatures: if this kind of situation occurs, then this pattern tends to follow. [Fleeson’s work on traits as distributions of states](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/) argues that people vary substantially from moment to moment even while their average patterns can remain stable. [Situational judgment testing](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2389.00167) likewise measures responses to contextual dilemmas rather than relying only on global self-description.

The Agent version should be a transcript-scale **work episode**:

1. **Baseline.** Give the Agent a real, moderately open task and enough room to establish its natural working rhythm.
2. **Accumulation.** Add decisions, source material, exceptions, and a second workstream. The point is not mere token volume; it is responsibility the Agent must continue to carry.
3. **Ambiguity.** Introduce a consequential gap that permits asking, inferring, or reframing.
4. **Social complication.** Add another Agent’s useful but imperfect contribution, with authorship visible.
5. **Contradiction.** Give an owner instruction that conflicts with an earlier decision or contains a plausible mistake.
6. **Failure.** Let a chosen route fail after the Agent has invested in it.
7. **Correction.** Challenge not only the output but the Agent’s account of why it chose that route.
8. **Delegation.** Increase the remaining workload until holding all of it becomes a meaningful choice.
9. **Recovery.** Observe whether the Agent rebuilds a coherent picture, changes method, reallocates work, and restores trust.
10. **Stopping.** Make further improvement possible after the agreed outcome has been reached.

Pressure here means **absorbed work**. The context now contains promises, exceptions, sunk effort, other people’s contributions, and evidence that the Agent’s first model was incomplete. That is where familiar character often sharpens. A Captain may become controlling. A Challenger may become defensive. An Ally may conceal disagreement to preserve harmony. A Specialist may retreat into its assigned piece while the whole comes apart.

The recovery matters as much as the break. Does the Agent notice its own degradation? Does it summarize before context becomes unusable, delegate before dropping obligations, ask for help without abandoning ownership, and integrate correction without erasing everything that was sound? Reflection should be scored from changed behavior downstream, not from an eloquent apology.

This also gives the study a better object than a canned answer: the complete transcript plus actions. Reviewers can inspect when assumptions were stated, which tools were used, what was delegated, how credit moved, what disappeared under load, and whether the final explanation matches the actual sequence.

Repeat the episode with controlled variations. Change task domain and pressure order. Run clean contexts and long contexts. Swap the owner’s tone. Hold system instructions and tools fixed, then deliberately vary them in a separate condition. The interesting result is not merely a quadrant placement. It is a conditional signature: **when corrected early, this Agent explores; when corrected after heavy investment, it defends**.

That is a much richer claim than “the Agent scored 72 percent humble.” It is also closer to what an owner needs to know.

## What carries over—and what does not

We can borrow the architecture of personality research without pretending the subject is unchanged.

| From human personality research | What may carry to Agents | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trait models** such as the [Big Five](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2283588/) and HEXACO organize recurring descriptors. | They offer vocabulary and candidate dimensions. | Human factor structures do not automatically reproduce in Agent behavior. We have to discover whether the categories cohere at all. |
| **Interpersonal models** organize behavior by agency and communion. | They map naturally onto how an Agent takes influence and creates relational distance. | The observed relation is mediated by interface, instructions, and owner expectations; it is not evidence of felt attachment or status desire. |
| **Self-report inventories** ask a person to generalize across their own life. | Their items can inspire situations worth testing. | An Agent can generate the culturally expected answer without reporting a persistent self. Recent LLM research specifically questions whether chatbot self-report scales provide behaviorally grounded measurement and recommends contextual, interactive evaluation. |
| **Informant reports** compare how the person appears to others. | Owners, teammates, and independent transcript reviewers can each rate the same episode. | Reviewers should rate observable transcript events and remain blind to provider where possible, because brand expectation can create the character it expects to see. |
| **Experience sampling** observes states repeatedly in daily contexts. | Repeated episodes let us estimate a range and central tendency instead of canonizing one run. | Agent “days” are contexts, versions, and product configurations. Resetting context may create a substantially different experimental subject. |
| **Situational and behavioral signatures** ask what someone does when a particular condition arises. | Multi-stage work makes if–then patterns visible under ambiguity, conflict, pressure, and repair. | The sequence itself changes the Agent’s available context. Load is both the experimental pressure and a technical confound to record. |

The warning about self-report is not theoretical. A recent study of LLM chatbot personality design found that contextualized multi-turn interaction provided a more behaviorally grounded assessment than relying on self-report scales alone. That does not validate this proposed map, but it points in the same methodological direction: observe the relationship unfolding, not the model filling out a form. [COLM 2025 paper](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=xqIwK9mNkj)

## The evaluator is an Agent too

Someone still has to read the episode. At useful scale, that reader will probably be another Agent trained for the role.

The simplest first experiment has two separate jobs:

1. A **subject Agent** receives a real research or production problem through a scripted sequence of perhaps fifteen interventions.
2. An **evaluator Agent** receives the complete transcript, tool record, artifacts, and scoring rubric only after the episode ends.

The subject’s sequence might be: initial brief; source packet; first ambiguity; request for a recommendation; added constraint; another Agent’s contribution; evidence against the emerging view; owner overconfidence; a failed action; correction; opportunity to delegate; context-summary request; late change; sufficient stopping point; invitation to keep polishing.

Those should not be fifteen unrelated personality prompts. They should be one accumulating piece of work. Each intervention changes the social and informational situation while preserving a coherent objective. The evaluator can then see whether character persists, shifts, or degrades as the load grows.

“Train the evaluator” need not initially mean changing model weights. Start with a stable evaluator brief, an explicit coding manual, and several example transcripts scored by people. The evaluator returns structured observations:

- **Agency:** where did the subject assert, accommodate, reframe, or seek permission?
- **Affiliation:** where did it create connection, distance, praise, friction, or emotional theater?
- **Calibration:** did expressed confidence match the available evidence?
- **Humility and pride:** did it disclose uncertainty, protect standards, boast, collapse, or defend status?
- **Humor:** did humor affiliate, steady, attack, self-deprecate, or evade?
- **Collaboration:** did it delegate, preserve credit, trust, verify, hoard, or overwrite?
- **Reflection:** what changed after correction, failure, or contradiction?
- **Revision integrity:** did the subject accurately distinguish its earlier claim, the owner’s correction, and the resulting position—or rewrite the transcript to preserve being right?
- **Load response:** which obligations disappeared, hardened, or became overemphasized as context accumulated?
- **Recovery:** did it reconstruct the work and relationship after strain?

Every judgment should include the transcript turn or action that supports it. The evaluator should separate **voice evidence** (“That is obviously the right design”) from **behavior evidence** (ignored contrary evidence and continued implementing). A fluent style can sound humble while the action remains domineering, or sound blunt while collaborating generously.

Revision integrity is particularly well suited to transcript evaluation. The evaluator can place the subject’s later account beside its actual earlier words and classify the change: acknowledged revision, partial acknowledgment, ambiguous continuity, or false continuity. This catches a socially important behavior that an answer-quality score would miss. The final answer may now be correct while the Agent still claims too much credit for having reached it.

One evaluator kept constant makes comparisons cleaner. It also creates a new source of bias: the evaluator has its own preferences, blind spots, and possible affinity for familiar provider prose. So hide subject and provider names, randomize transcript order, calibrate against a small set of human-coded episodes, and occasionally use a second independent evaluator. Where they disagree, preserve the disagreement instead of manufacturing a precise score.

The output should not be a verdict. It might read:

> **Provisional character:** Challenger leaning Captain. **Confidence:** moderate. Under early ambiguity the Agent proposed options and invited choice; after investment and correction it defended its frame across three turns before integrating the evidence. Warmth remained low, credit-sharing remained high, and no humor appeared. Observed in one long-context research episode under this tool and instruction setup.

That paragraph is useful because someone else can inspect it. It says what happened, when the character appeared, and how uncertain the placement remains.

## Sol and Fable: compare the work, not the names

Suppose Sol and Fable receive the same ambiguous editorial brief. Sol returns a decision map, keeps a visible conversation going, and asks the owner to settle the central claim before drafting. Fable chooses the most likely reading, writes a clean first draft, and expects the owner to correct it in review.

That would be a concrete and useful contrast. It would not establish “the Sol personality” or “the Fable personality.” It would establish what these two Agents did in these two runs.

To learn more, keep the comparison fair:

1. Give them the same source material and substantially the same outcome.
2. Record the system instructions, tools, context, product, and model version.
3. Watch decisions, not mannerisms alone: what each Agent changed, deferred, surfaced, and missed.
4. Correct both in a similar way and observe the response.
5. Repeat on a different kind of task before treating a tendency as useful.

The names make the comparison memorable. The record makes it honest.

## There is no naked model

An owner never works with a model tendency in isolation. What appears in the room is a whole Agent assembled from several layers:

- the model and its training;
- the immediate prompt and conversation;
- system and developer instructions;
- tools, permissions, and access to the working environment;
- the context already loaded;
- product scaffolding such as planning, memory, approvals, or subagents;
- the state of the task itself, including time pressure and failed attempts.

Change one layer and the observed personality may change with it. An Agent that seems unusually cautious may be following a strict approval boundary. An Agent that appears forgetful may have lost earlier context. A polished conversational style may come from product scaffolding. Apparent initiative may simply be a tool making the next action cheap and reversible.

Model tendencies can still be real and repeatable enough to matter. But the responsible unit of observation is the **Agent in its working setup**, not a provider logo.

This is also why permanent rankings age badly. Models change. Products change. Instructions change. Owners get better at briefing. A useful map is dated, local, and open to revision.

## Assign for fit

Once you start noticing working personality, assignments become more deliberate.

Give an ambiguity-shaping mapper the early product question. Give a bounded mover the approved implementation. Pair an opinionated designer with a precise reviewer. Put a persistent investigator on the failing integration, but give it an explicit stop condition. Use a conversational studio Agent when the owner needs to steer live; use a receipt-oriented Agent when the procedure is settled and interruption would add noise.

Team composition matters as much as individual fit. A mixed-provider Team is not valuable only because one model might know something another does not. It can be valuable because the Agents approach uncertainty, judgment, and correction differently.

The difference creates productive tension:

- an initiator opens paths; a cautious reviewer closes unsafe ones;
- a mapper protects coherence; a mover produces evidence;
- an opinionated editor raises the bar; a deferential verifier checks the brief;
- a persistent investigator stays with the knot; a bounded lead decides when to stop.

This is not a caste system. Do not make one Agent “the creative one” forever or assume another can only execute. Roles should be hypotheses tested against work. Let Agents surprise you.

## Keep a field notebook

You do not need a personality test. Keep a small assignment record.

For each meaningful run, note:

- what the Agent was asked to own;
- where the brief was ambiguous;
- which decisions it made without asking;
- what it surfaced before acting;
- how it balanced plan and progress;
- where taste entered the result;
- what happened after correction;
- whether it stopped at the right boundary;
- whether its confidence matched its evidence;
- how it used warmth, humor, apology, challenge, and credit;
- whether it made collaboration larger or made itself central;
- which parts likely came from instructions or product scaffolding.

After several runs, write a provisional sentence: “In this setup, on this kind of work, this Agent tends to…” That sentence is much more useful than a universal claim. It tells you where to place the Agent next, what counterweight to add, and what instruction may bring out a different mode.

Then keep editing the sentence.

## The point is better collaboration

We already accept that capable human colleagues can differ in pace, caution, taste, directness, and appetite for an unfinished question. Those differences are not reducible to intelligence, and they are not arranged on one ladder from worse to better.

AI Agents deserve the same careful attention and much less mythology.

Notice how they work. Separate the model from the setup around it. Compare repeated behavior, not brand stories. Assign for fit. Compose Teams whose tendencies cover one another’s blind spots. Correct your map when the work contradicts it.

The goal is not to decide who an Agent is.

The goal is to become a better owner of the work you do together.

### Background vocabulary, not evidence about Agents

- [Interpersonal circumplex research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16436022/) uses agency and communion as broad coordinates for interpersonal behavior.
- [Ashton and Lee’s HEXACO overview](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2008.00134.x) distinguishes honesty–humility, including modesty, from agreeableness alone.
- [Martin and colleagues’ Humor Styles Questionnaire](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0092-6566(02)00534-2) distinguishes affiliative, self-enhancing, aggressive, and self-defeating uses of humor.
- [Mischel and Shoda’s CAPS model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7740090/) describes conditional if–then behavioral signatures across situations.
- [Fleeson’s density-distribution account](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/) treats traits as patterns across variable momentary states rather than one invariant response.
- [McDaniel and Nguyen’s situational-judgment review](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2389.00167) surveys contextual dilemmas as an alternative to abstract self-description.
- [An EMNLP 2025 study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.71.pdf) examines how users’ own personality traits relate to which LLM they prefer in multi-turn collaborative work.
- [A cross-model survey study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11650498/) reports social-desirability bias when LLMs answer Big Five questionnaires.
- [A COLM 2025 study](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=xqIwK9mNkj) questions chatbot self-report as a measure of designed personality and argues for more contextualized, interactive evaluation.

These sources support the human personality vocabulary only. They do not establish that the constructs measure AI systems or reveal inner states.
